You already know candy and soda are bad for your teeth. But some foods that seem harmless — or even healthy — are quietly eroding your enamel, feeding cavity-causing bacteria, and staining your teeth.

Here are 7 common foods and drinks that are doing more damage than you think, and what to eat instead.

## 1. Citrus Fruits and Juices

Yes, oranges and grapefruits are packed with vitamin C. But they're also extremely acidic, with a pH between 2.0 and 3.5 — well below the 5.5 threshold where enamel starts to dissolve.

Orange juice is particularly damaging because you swish it around your mouth as you drink. Studies show that regular citrus juice consumption increases enamel erosion risk by 37%.

**What to do instead:** Eat citrus with meals (saliva production during eating neutralizes acid). Drink juice through a straw. Wait 30 minutes before brushing — brushing acid-softened enamel causes more damage.

## 2. Dried Fruits

Raisins, dried cranberries, and dried apricots seem like healthy snack alternatives. But they're essentially sugar bombs that stick to your teeth.

Fresh grapes have about 15g of sugar per cup. Raisins? 86g per cup. The sticky texture means the sugar clings to tooth surfaces and between teeth for hours, feeding decay-causing bacteria the entire time.

**What to do instead:** Choose fresh fruit over dried. If you do eat dried fruit, rinse your mouth with water immediately afterward and floss within an hour.

## 3. Sports and Energy Drinks

These are often marketed as healthy hydration options, but they combine high sugar content with extreme acidity. A study in the journal *General Dentistry* found that energy drinks cause twice as much enamel damage as sports drinks.

The acidity level of popular energy drinks ranges from pH 2.7 to 3.3 — comparable to battery acid (pH 1.0) in relative terms.

**What to do instead:** Hydrate with water. If you need electrolytes after exercise, coconut water is less acidic and sugar-free options are available.

## 4. Crackers and Chips

Refined starches like crackers, chips, and white bread break down into simple sugars almost instantly in your mouth. Worse, the chewed-up paste gets pressed into the grooves of your molars and between teeth.

Bacteria feast on these trapped starches for hours, producing the acid that causes cavities. Crackers can be worse than candy for this reason — candy dissolves, but cracker paste sticks.

**What to do instead:** Choose whole-grain alternatives, which break down slower. Rinse with water after snacking. Pair starchy snacks with cheese (which neutralizes acid).

## 5. Ice

Chewing ice doesn't introduce sugar or acid, but the mechanical damage can be severe. Ice chewing is one of the top causes of cracked teeth, broken fillings, and damaged dental work seen by dentists.

The extreme temperature also causes microscopic fractures in enamel that worsen over time.

**What to do instead:** If you love crunching, try baby carrots or apple slices. If you chew ice compulsively, mention it to your doctor — it can be a sign of iron deficiency.

## 6. Coffee

Coffee itself has a pH of about 5.0 — mildly acidic. The bigger problems are what people add to it (sugar, flavored syrups) and its staining effect.

Coffee contains tannins that cause chromogens to stick to enamel. Over time, this creates the characteristic yellow-brown staining that's difficult to remove with brushing alone.

Drinking coffee throughout the day is worse than drinking it all at once, because you're constantly re-bathing your teeth in acid and staining compounds.

**What to do instead:** Drink coffee in one sitting rather than sipping all day. Use a straw for iced coffee. Rinse with water after your last sip. Consider whitening maintenance products.

## 7. Alcohol

Wine, beer, and spirits all reduce saliva production. Saliva is your mouth's natural defense system — it washes away food particles, neutralizes acid, and delivers minerals that repair early enamel damage.

A dry mouth lets bacteria thrive. Red wine adds heavy staining on top of this, and mixed drinks add sugar.

**What to do instead:** Alternate alcoholic drinks with water. Chew sugar-free gum after drinking to stimulate saliva flow. Avoid brushing immediately after — wait at least 30 minutes.

## Foods That STRENGTHEN Your Teeth

Balance the damage with these tooth-friendly options:

- **Cheese** — Raises mouth pH, provides calcium, stimulates saliva
- **Leafy greens** — High in calcium and folic acid
- **Almonds** — Calcium and protein with low sugar
- **Yogurt** — Probiotics fight cavity-causing bacteria
- **Celery and carrots** — Act as natural toothbrushes, stimulate saliva
- **Green tea** — Contains fluoride and polyphenols that fight bacteria
- **Water** — The best thing you can drink for your teeth

## The Bottom Line

You don't need to eliminate all these foods. The key is awareness and damage control:

1. Eat acidic foods with meals, not as standalone snacks
2. Rinse with water after eating problematic foods
3. Wait 30 minutes before brushing after acidic foods
4. Use fluoride toothpaste to help remineralize enamel
5. Chew sugar-free xylitol gum after meals

Your diet shapes your dental health as much as your brushing habits. Small changes in when and how you eat can make a significant difference.
